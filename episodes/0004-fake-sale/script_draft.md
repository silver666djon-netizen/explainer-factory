# NARRATION
## COLD OPEN
A hair dryer at Boots. Was ⏸ forty-nine ninety-nine. Now ⏸ eighteen ninety-nine. In the twelve months before, it had never cost forty-nine ninety-nine there. Not once. Larry is made up. The forty-nine ninety-nine was too.
Case file number three. The sale that never was.
Suspect number one. Name: the "was" price. Aliases: original, regular, compare at. Last seen: every Black Friday. It never touches your money. It just tells you how much you're saving.
## MONDAY 9:00 AM: FAIR AND SQUARE
Monday, nine a.m. Larry reads about a store that tried to arrest this suspect.
In 2011, J.C. Penney ran ⏸ five hundred and ninety different promotions. Only ⏸ zero point two percent of its revenue came from full-price sales. Almost three-quarters came from things marked half off or more.
So in 2012 a new boss, Ron Johnson, the man behind the Apple Store, tore it all up. No fake markdowns. No coupons. One honest price on every tag. He called it Fair and Square.
"People are disgusted," he said, "with the lack of integrity on pricing."
Honest prices. The customers were not ready for the experience.
That year, sales fell by ⏸ a quarter. The company lost ⏸ nine hundred and eighty-five million dollars.
Even before Johnson was fired in April 2013, the old game was back. A five-dollar T-shirt got a six-dollar tag, so it could be marked down to five again. The T-shirt did not change. The tag got promoted.
The store that stopped lying nearly went broke. Our suspect doesn't work alone.
## TUESDAY 7:40 PM: ALWAYS ON SALE
Tuesday, seven-forty p.m. Larry wants a stand mixer. And it's on sale. Of course it is.
First, the evidence. Britain's Which? checked ⏸ two hundred and twenty-seven Black Friday deals. Nine in ten were the same price, or cheaper, at other times of the year.
Now Larry's mixer. Consumer Reports priced this exact one at Kohl's: on sale for ⏸ four eighty-nine ninety-nine. "Regular" price: five forty-nine ninety-nine. On Amazon, the same mixer: ⏸ three ninety-nine ninety-nine. Larry's "saving": sixty dollars. His real overpay: ⏸ ninety.
So how regular is regular? One consumer advocate tracked twenty Kohl's items for a hundred and three days. They were on sale ⏸ eighty-six percent of the time. One in four never sold at its regular price at all. Regular price: the thing that happens on the other fourteen percent of days.
Years later, Checkbook magazine tracked nineteen big retailers. Most of the time, the "sale" price simply was the price.
A federal appeals court summed it up in three words: price advertisements matter.
Los Angeles sued four department-store chains over fake "original" prices. Five years later, the city dropped its case against Kohl's and Macy's, after the judge tore into its expert witness.
Turns out, proving a price was never real is harder than faking one.
## WHY YOUR BRAIN SAYS YES
Why does a made-up number work? Psychologists once spun a rigged wheel that stopped on ten or sixty-five, then asked people to guess a percentage. The sixty-five group guessed almost twice as high.
At MIT, students wrote down the last digits of their social security number, then bid on a keyboard. High numbers bid ⏸ fifty-six dollars. Low numbers, sixteen. A later replication found the effect much weaker. Weaker, but not zero.
And Harvard researchers found outlet shoppers paid about seventy-seven cents more for every extra dollar of "original" price.

## IN DEFENCE OF THE ACCUSED
In defence of the accused.
One: Penney tried honesty, and shoppers walked out. They wanted the hunt.
Two: a sale can still be a deal. An FTC study checked twenty department-store "regular" prices. Twelve were higher than any rival charged. Yet the sale prices averaged about four percent below the competition.
Three: nobody buys a mattress every month. Waiting for a sale is smart.
The "was" price may be fiction. The saving isn't always. And the accomplice nobody arrests? Our love of a bargain.
## FRIDAY 11:58 PM: THE CLOCK THAT RESETS
Friday, eleven fifty-eight p.m. Larry is buying a mattress. Sale ends in two minutes.
Suspect number two. Name: the countdown clock. Aliases: "sale ends in two fifty-nine fifty-nine." "Only three left."
In Britain, the competition watchdog took mattress-maker Emma to court. The judgment has the numbers. ⏸ Ninety-eight point two percent of Emma's sales were products shown with a struck-through "was" price. In 2024 it sold seven discounted mattresses for every one at full price. One model, in one test period, sold just ⏸ zero point zero two percent at full price. At full price, this mattress is rarer than a quiet Black Friday.
The watchdog wanted a simple rule: for every two sold on sale, one at full price. In July 2026, the High Court found Emma had broken the law in places, but refused the ratio. A fixed rule, the judge said, risked drawing the line in the wrong place.
The clock lost anyway. In May, Emma agreed its countdown timers must end when the sale really ends, and can't be reused on a similar deal within four weeks. The clock resets. Your decision doesn't.
So what makes a "was" price real? America's rulebook says: a price you really offered, regularly, for a reasonably substantial time. Its own example is a pen tagged ten dollars for a few days, just so seven-fifty can look like a bargain. California says: within the last three months. And in the EU, any "was" price must be the lowest of the last thirty days.
## SATURDAY 10:00 AM: THREE CHECKS
Saturday, ten a.m. Three checks before you pay.
One. Was it ever that price here? Look for a date. If the tag can't give you one, treat the "was" as decoration.
Two. Check the price history. Free trackers show what a product really cost, week by week: Keepa or camelcamelcamel for Amazon in the US, PriceSpy or idealo in Britain and Europe. A "was" price that never shows up on the chart is a suspect.
Three. Find the floor. In the EU, the "was" price must be the thirty-day low. Everywhere else, search the exact model number. Larry's mixer: ninety dollars cheaper, one click away.
Forget the percentage. Compare the price you'd pay with the price you could pay.

Case closed.

The sentence: a date on every tag, and a second look from you.
